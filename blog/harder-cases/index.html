2. Open challenges

Four challenges stay open.

  • Generality of the spec. Lesson, from Post 05. The spec requires a rejected write to preserve the whole store, on every rejection path. Tests cover only the paths they are written for. Challenge. Writing specs for whole failure classes, and knowing how much generality is enough.
  • Model-to-code gap. Lesson, from Posts 03 and 04. The proof covers only part of the code, and each part rests on stated assumptions. The refinement covers the translated write method under stated representation and library contract assumptions. The binary, the concrete standard library, the whole lifecycle, and allocation sit outside it. Challenge. Widen what the proof covers and keep every assumption explicit and accounted for.

Binary level, for contrast. Anthropic and Theorem prove compiled binaries instead of source. A binary proof covers the compiler’s output, so no translation gap sits between the proof and the artifact that runs. The trusted base moves down a level, to the instruction-set model and the process model. Their spec is a fuzz obligation restated as a proof. The entry point never crashes. The spec in this series is hand-written and states properties directly, the exact effect of a write and the lifetime of an identity. Their proofs are machine-written and run to hundreds of thousands of lines. The proofs here fit in files a reader can finish. A proof at either level holds inside its stated machine and environment model.

  • Inputs with structure. Lesson, from the toy. Its guards are direct comparisons and offsets get no arithmetic, which is why representability stayed manageable. Challenge. Inputs with structure, parsers and length fields, where untrusted inputs get arithmetic and narrowing before any check.
  • Keeping proof alive. Lesson, from section 1. On a toy, spec, model, translation, and checks stayed in step by hand. Challenge. The cost of keeping specs and proofs in step with code that changes every week.
